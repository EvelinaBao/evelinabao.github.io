## The question

Elections aren't just political events — for many people they're a source of sustained personal stress. How do Americans actually cope with a high-stakes election as it unfolds, how does that differ across demographic groups, and what helps protect well-being?

## What I did

I led a national panel study tracking 1,100+ Americans across five weekly waves surrounding the 2024 US election, collecting 5,000+ responses. I developed predictive models of engagement, well-being, and emotional regulation, using multilevel models to separate within-person change over the election from stable between-person differences, and compared coping strategies across racial and demographic groups.

## What I found

Election-related stress is real but unequally distributed. The election was generally associated with higher negative emotions and worse well-being but the patterns were different across different political and racial groups. 

For political groups, Democrats’ negative emotions increased and well-being decreased even more after the election results were revealed, whereas Republicans' positive emotions decreased and well-being increased after the election results were revealed. For racial group differences, racial minorities had lower negative emotions than White dominant group during the election period, showing racial minorities resilience.

Successfully used emotion regulation strategies, including reappraisal, distraction, suppression, and acknowledgement predicted lower negative emotion. However, the beneficial effect of reappraisal was weaker for racial minorities, suggesting election is one kind of stressor that is hard for racial minorities to find its silver lining.  

## The impact

The study produced data-driven recommendations for emotion interventions to support well-being across demographics — and a template for panel research on any recurring, population-level stressor. Presented as flash talks and posters at APS, SAS, SPSP, and the Princeton Summit on Affective Science.
